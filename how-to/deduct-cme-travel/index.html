How to deduct medical conference travel

Last reviewed 10 August 2026 · Sources: IRS Publication 463 (2025) and the Internal Revenue Code

This is general information, not tax advice. Tax outcomes depend on your specific facts, your entity structure and your state. Nothing here creates a client relationship, and convene.md is not a tax adviser. Rules also change — the employee deduction below changed on 1 January 2026. Confirm anything that matters with your own CPA or tax attorney before you file.

Almost every physician pays for conference travel one of two ways: an employer writes the cheque, or you write it yourself and deduct it. The two paths have almost nothing in common. One is governed by your employer's HR policy; the other by the Internal Revenue Code. Getting them confused is how people end up out of pocket.

If you are employed

Your employer's policy is the whole game

As of 1 January 2026 there is no tax fallback. If your employer does not reimburse it, you cannot deduct it. Reimbursement is not the easier route — it is the only route.

Unreimbursed employee business expenses were suspended as miscellaneous itemized deductions from 2018 onward. That suspension was scheduled to lapse after 2025, but section 70110 of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the disallowance permanent beginning in 2026. Publication 463 puts the underlying rule plainly:

“…unreimbursed employee travel expense as a miscellaneous itemized deduction due to the suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions that are subject to the 2% floor under section 67(a). The suspension applies to tax years beginning after 2017.” IRS Publication 463 (2025)

Narrow exceptions survive for Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, and fee-basis state or local government officials, who deduct as an adjustment to income rather than as an itemized deduction. Almost no practising physician qualifies.

Know your policy before you book

Policies vary enormously between systems. Get answers in writing to:

  • The amount, and the period. Many allowances run on a fiscal year, not a calendar year — a "use it or lose it" balance can expire in June or September.
  • Whether it rolls over. Some systems let you bank two years to fund one bigger trip.
  • What it covers beyond registration: airfare, lodging, meals, ground transport, licensure and board fees, journal subscriptions, society dues, review courses.
  • Pre-approval. Many plans require sign-off before you book. Approval after the fact is often refused outright.
  • Booking channel. Some employers only reimburse travel booked through a designated agency or corporate card.
  • Paid time. Whether conference days come out of CME days, PTO, or neither — a separate question from the money, and easy to forget until it's too late.

Accountable plan, or taxable income

This is the part almost nobody checks, and it decides whether reimbursement reaches you tax-free. To be an accountable plan, your employer's arrangement must meet all three of these:

“Your expenses must have a business connection… You must adequately account to your employer for these expenses within a reasonable period of time. You must return any excess reimbursement or allowance within a reasonable period of time.” IRS Publication 463 (2025), Accountable Plans

Meet all three and the reimbursement is not wages. Fail any of them — most often by a flat "CME stipend" paid with no receipts required, or by not returning an unspent advance — and the payment becomes a nonaccountable plan: it lands on your W-2 as taxable wages. And because you can no longer deduct employee business expenses, you cannot offset it. A no-receipts stipend feels generous and quietly costs you your marginal rate.

Keep and submit receipts

  • Keep documentary evidence — a receipt showing amount, date, place and character of the expense.
  • The IRS's own threshold: documentary evidence is not required where “your expense, other than lodging, is less than $75.” Lodging always needs a receipt, at any amount. Your employer may be stricter, and their rule governs your reimbursement.
  • A hotel folio should separate lodging, meals and telephone charges — an undifferentiated total is weaker evidence.
  • Submit within your plan's window. "Reasonable period of time" is an accountable-plan requirement, not a courtesy.
  • Keep the CME certificate too. Claiming credit and claiming money are separate processes with separate deadlines.
If you are self-employed or 1099

You are deducting it yourself, on Schedule C

The trip must be away from your tax home, and attendance must benefit your trade or business. Get those two right and most of the rest follows.

Your tax home is “the entire city or general area where your main place of business or work is located,” regardless of where your family home is. You are travelling away from home when duties require you to be away from that area substantially longer than an ordinary work day and you need sleep or rest.

“You can deduct your travel expenses when you attend a convention if you can show that your attendance benefits your trade or business. You can’t deduct the travel expenses for your family.” IRS Publication 463 (2025), Conventions

Keep the agenda. Publication 463 says the programme “generally shows the purpose of the convention,” and that you show benefit “by comparing the agenda with the official duties and responsibilities of your position.” For a physician attending a specialty conference this is rarely a hard argument — but it is an argument you should be able to make on paper years later.

Adding a holiday to a domestic conference

If the trip is primarily business, you deduct the cost of getting there and back plus your business-related costs on the ground — but not the extra days. Publication 463's own worked example: a nine-day trip costing $2,165 where only six days were business yields a $1,633.50 deduction, the cost the trip would have been without the detour.

If the trip is primarily personal, the calculus flips hard: “the entire cost of the trip is a nondeductible personal expense.” You may still deduct qualifying expenses incurred at the destination — the registration fee — but the airfare is gone. For a one-day course bolted onto a two-week holiday, expect to lose the flight.

Records

  • Keep a written record — an account book, log or expense statement — plus receipts. Written beats recollection; a record kept on a computer counts.
  • Receipts required except where the expense, other than lodging, is under $75.
  • Keep the conference agenda and your CME certificate with the receipts.
  • Self-employed physicians report on Schedule C (or Schedule F).

Which days count — including your flight days

This is the question that comes up most, and the answer is more generous than most physicians assume. It matters for self-employed physicians directly; for employed physicians it shapes what a reimbursement policy will tolerate.

Your travel days count. They do not need to be educational.

“Count as a business day any day you spend traveling to or from a business destination. However, if because of a nonbusiness activity you don’t travel by a direct route, your business days are the days it would take you to travel a reasonably direct route to your business destination.” IRS Publication 463 (2025), Transportation day

So the day your flight lands is a business day even though you attend nothing. The same applies to the day you fly home. The catch is only the indirect route: fly to Kyoto via three days in Seoul and those extra days are yours, not the government's.

Days your presence is required count — even mostly idle ones

“Count as a business day any day your presence is required at a particular place for a specific business purpose. Count it as a business day even if you spend most of the day on nonbusiness activities.” IRS Publication 463 (2025), Presence required

A gap day inside the conference usually counts. A day after it does not.

“Count weekends, holidays, and other necessary standby days as business days if they fall between business days. But if they follow your business meetings or activity and you remain at your business destination for nonbusiness or personal reasons, don’t count them as business days.” IRS Publication 463 (2025), Certain weekends and holidays

The IRS's own illustration: a meeting in Quebec on a Friday and another the following Monday makes both days business days — “Because the weekend is between business days, Saturday and Sunday are counted as business days. This is true even though you use the weekend” for other things. The structural lesson is simple: a free day sandwiched between sessions is a business day; the identical day tacked on the end is not. Sequence, not activity, decides it.

Stacking two conferences to cover the weekend

The sandwich rule above is not just a technicality to know about — it is something you can plan around, and it is the single biggest lever on a CME trip. If a weekend falls between two business days it counts as business days. So two conferences in the same city, one ending Friday and the next starting Monday, turn the Saturday and Sunday in between from personal days into business days.

Book them as two separate trips and you pay for two sets of flights, and the weekend at home is nobody's business day. Book them as one trip and you pay for one set of flights, and the weekend in between is covered.

What makes this work — and what breaks it

  • You have to actually attend both. The rule counts days between business days. If the second meeting is a fig leaf you skip, the weekend was never sandwiched and the whole structure fails.
  • Both must genuinely relate to your practice. Attendance has to benefit your trade or business — that test applies to each meeting independently, not to the trip as a whole.
  • Same destination is cleanest. If the two are in different cities you are not relying on the sandwich rule at all: the day you travel between them is already a business day as a transportation day.
  • Order matters more than content. Two conferences with a weekend after the last one is an ordinary trip plus a personal weekend. The identical days, moved between the meetings, are business days.
  • Employed physicians: this is a policy question, not a tax one. The IRS day count does not obligate your employer to pay for the Saturday hotel night. But a single trip covering two approved conferences is an easier expense claim than two separate trips, and one airfare instead of two is an argument most CME administrators will take.

One caution on foreign trips: extra days abroad feed the counting tests in the section above. Stacking two meetings overseas can push you past a week outside the United States, at which point the 25% test governs instead of the seven-day rule. Count before you book.

Where the conference is held changes the rules

This trips people up because the boundary is not "the United States" and it is not "abroad." The IRS uses a defined North American area, and it is much wider than the name suggests — it includes a long list of Caribbean and Pacific destinations.

Inside the North American area

Ordinary travel rules apply. Per Publication 463, the area includes:

American Samoa · Antigua and Barbuda · Aruba · Bahamas · Baker Island · Barbados · Bermuda · Canada · Costa Rica · Curaçao · Dominica · Dominican Republic · Grenada · Guam · Guyana · Honduras · Howland Island · Jamaica · Jarvis Island · Johnston Island · Kingman Reef · Marshall Islands · Mexico · Micronesia · Midway Islands · Northern Mariana Islands · Palau · Palmyra Atoll · Panama · Puerto Rico · Saint Lucia · Trinidad and Tobago · USA · U.S. Virgin Islands · Wake Island

Outside it, you clear two extra hurdles

“…unless: The meeting is directly related to the active conduct of your trade or business, and It is as reasonable to hold the meeting outside the North American area as within the North American area.” IRS Publication 463 (2025), Conventions Held Outside the North American Area

The reasonableness test weighs the sponsoring organisation's purpose and activities, where its members live, and where its other meetings have been held. A genuinely international society rotating its world congress through Europe or Asia has a strong case. A US-focused course that chose Tuscany has a weaker one.

What this means for destinations on this site

The distinction cuts across places that feel similar. Of the Caribbean and Latin American destinations in our listings:

  • Inside the area — ordinary rules: Bahamas, Aruba, Costa Rica, Mexico, Canada, Puerto Rico
  • Outside it — reasonableness test applies: Cayman Islands, Saint Kitts and Nevis, and every European and Asian destination including Kyoto, Greece, Spain and Italy

Two islands a short flight apart can land on opposite sides of this line. The list is set by statute and updated by revenue ruling, so verify the current position for your destination and tax year rather than relying on a list you read once.

Foreign trips: two exceptions worth planning around

For travel outside the United States, a trip that mixes business and personal time can still be considered entirely for business. Publication 463: “You can deduct all your travel expenses of getting to and from your business destination if your trip is entirely for business or considered entirely for business.” Two of the four exceptions matter to conference-goers:

ExceptionTestCounting quirk
Outside the US a week or less 7 consecutive days or fewer outside the United States “don’t count the day you leave the United States, but do count the day you return”
Under 25% personal More than a week outside the US, but under 25% of total time on nonbusiness activity Count both the first and last day of the trip

The IRS's own example for the second: 14 business days and 5 personal days in Tokyo, plus a flying day each way, is 5/21 — under 25% — so the round-trip airfare is deductible, along with 16 days of lodging and meals.

The cruise conference trap

CME cruises are marketed hard to physicians, and the tax treatment is far more restrictive than the brochures suggest. There is a hard annual cap:

“You can deduct up to $2,000 per year of your expenses of attending conventions, seminars, or similar meetings held on cruise ships. All ships that sail are considered cruise ships.” IRS Publication 463 (2025), Cruise Ships

And the cap only applies if all of the following hold:

  • the meeting is directly related to the active conduct of your trade or business;
  • the ship is a vessel registered in the United States;
  • all of the ship's ports of call are in the United States or its territories; and
  • you attach two signed written statements to your return — one from you covering trip days and hours of scheduled business activity, one from the sponsoring organisation with the daily programme.

The registration requirement is the one that bites. The great majority of cruise ships serving the US market are registered outside the United States, and a single foreign port of call independently breaks the test. Before assuming a cruise course is deductible at all, check the specific ship's flag and its full itinerary. Note also the separate luxury water travel rule, which caps deductions at twice the highest federal per diem rate per day.

Worked example: a real conference on this site

Internal Medicine for Primary Care, Kyoto — 10–13 November 2026. Say you are self-employed, you leave the US on 8 November and fly home arriving 15 November, and you spend the 14th sightseeing.

  • Days outside the US: 9–15 November. The departure day is not counted; the return day is. That is 7 days — “a week or less.”
  • Result: the trip is considered entirely for business under the seven-day exception, so the airfare is deductible even though the 14th was personal.
  • But: Kyoto is outside the North American area, so you must also show the meeting was directly related to your business and that holding it there was as reasonable as holding it in North America.
  • And: your spouse's ticket is not deductible, whatever the day count says.

Change one fact — fly home on the 17th instead — and you are over a week outside the US. Now the 25% test governs, and three trailing personal days out of ten is 30%. The cheaper trip, on paper, becomes the more expensive one.

Common questions

Does the day my flight lands have to include something educational?

No. A travel day is a business day in its own right: “Count as a business day any day you spend traveling to or from a business destination.” The conference does not need to have started.

The conference has a free afternoon — does that break the deduction?

No. A day counts as a business day if your presence is required for a business purpose, “even if you spend most of the day on nonbusiness activities.” And a full free day between session days is counted as a business day under the weekends-and-holidays rule.

Can I attend two conferences in one trip to cover the weekend between them?

Yes, and it is the most effective way to structure a CME trip — see stacking two conferences above. A meeting ending Friday and a second starting Monday in the same city makes the Saturday and Sunday business days, on one airfare instead of two. You do have to genuinely attend both, and each must benefit your practice.

Can I stay the weekend after the meeting ends?

You can, but those days are not business days: the rule explicitly excludes days that “follow your business meetings or activity” where you stay on for personal reasons. Lodging and meals for those days are not deductible, and for a domestic trip enough of them can flip the whole trip to “primarily personal” and cost you the airfare.

I'm employed and my hospital won't cover a course I want. Can I deduct it?

Not as an employee. The suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions became permanent in 2026, so there is no personal deduction to fall back on. If you also have 1099 income and the course genuinely relates to that business activity, it may be deductible against it — that is a question for your CPA, not a workaround to assume.

My employer pays a flat CME stipend with no receipts required. Is that better?

Usually worse. A stipend paid without accounting for expenses generally fails the accountable-plan rules, which makes it taxable wages on your W-2 — and you can no longer deduct the expenses to offset it. A reimbursement arrangement that requires receipts typically leaves you with more money.

Are my spouse's flights deductible if they attend the social programme?

No. Publication 463 is blunt: “You can’t deduct the travel expenses for your family.” A spouse's costs become deductible only in narrow circumstances where they are a bona fide employee travelling for a genuine business purpose.

Do I need a receipt for every coffee?

No. Documentary evidence is not required where the expense, other than lodging, is under $75 — though you still need a written record of amount, date, place and business purpose. Lodging always needs a receipt. If you are claiming from an employer, their policy may be stricter than the IRS.
